What Is Gamma Exposure (GEX) and Why Every Trader Should Know It

OptionsFlow Foundations 8 min read

Every options trader has heard the word gamma. Most know it loosely as "the rate at which delta changes." But there is a deeper and far more practical idea hiding behind this number — one that can explain why large indices sometimes move smoothly, why they occasionally spiral, and why certain price levels seem to hold with almost magnetic force. That idea is Gamma Exposure, or GEX.

The Market Maker in the Middle

To understand GEX, you first need to understand who is on the other side of the options you buy and sell. In the Indian F&O market, the overwhelming majority of retail and institutional options activity is absorbed by professional market makers — entities whose job is to provide continuous two-way quotes and earn the bid-ask spread. These market makers do not have a view on market direction. They are not bulls or bears. They are in the business of collecting premium while staying as close to delta-neutral as possible.

The critical consequence of this is that market makers must hedge their books continuously. When you buy a call option, the market maker who sold it to you becomes short that call. To stay neutral, they buy some amount of the underlying — specifically, an amount equal to the option's delta. If the market moves up, the delta of your call increases, and the market maker must buy more. If the market falls, delta shrinks and they must sell. This continuous hedging is called delta hedging, and the speed at which those hedge ratios change is governed entirely by gamma.

What GEX Actually Measures

Gamma Exposure is a measure of how much additional buying or selling a market maker will be forced to do in response to a 1% move in the underlying. More precisely, it estimates the total rupee value of delta-hedge adjustments triggered by a given price change, aggregated across all strikes and all open contracts.

Simple definition: GEX tells you how many crores worth of futures or spot a market maker will need to buy or sell if Nifty moves by 1%.

When this number is positive (market makers are net long gamma), they will buy when the market falls and sell when it rises — acting as a natural stabiliser. When this number is negative (market makers are net short gamma), they will sell when the market falls and buy when it rises — amplifying moves in both directions.

Long Gamma vs Short Gamma: Two Very Different Markets

The sign of aggregate GEX has a measurable impact on intraday and multiday price behaviour.

In a long gamma environment, markets tend to mean-revert. Sharp moves get absorbed quickly because the hedging flow is counter-directional to price. Implied volatility often drifts lower because the supply of gamma from market-maker hedging is large relative to demand. Range-bound strategies like iron condors and short straddles perform well in these conditions.

In a short gamma environment, markets can trend aggressively with no natural brake. Because market-maker hedging is now pro-cyclical (they sell into a falling market, buy into a rising one), small moves can become large ones. Realised volatility tends to be higher. Trending strategies and long options positions become more attractive.

GEX Pin Levels

Where total GEX is largest in absolute terms — the so-called GEX Pin — is a strike price where market-maker gamma is so concentrated that their hedging activity creates a strong gravitational pull. The reason is simple: as the market approaches this strike, the delta-hedge adjustments required become large and bidirectional, which means the market maker is forced to buy as price dips below and sell as price rises above. The net effect is magnetic: price is continuously pulled toward the pin.

This is why so many weekly expiry cycles end with Nifty closing extremely close to a round number or a heavily loaded strike. It is not random. It is the mechanical consequence of delta hedging at scale.

The Four Dealer Scenarios

Market makers can be long or short on both calls and puts simultaneously. The GEX chart on this dashboard presents four scenarios — different assumptions about which way market makers are positioned on each side of the book:

  • Long Call, Short Put — The most common real-world setup. Market makers buy calls from directional traders and sell puts to hedgers. This is the standard institutional posture.
  • Short Call, Short Put — Net short gamma across both sides. Amplifying environment.
  • Long Call, Long Put — Market makers long vol on both sides. Very dampening.
  • Short Call, Long Put — Bearish/hedge posture; directional bias in puts.

Each scenario produces a different GEX chart, and the truth often lies somewhere between them. The Probable GEX chart attempts to identify which scenario is most likely at each individual strike by analysing how open interest has been accumulating over recent sessions.

Key Takeaway

GEX is not a trading signal in the traditional sense — it doesn't tell you whether to buy or sell. What it does is describe the structural environment the market is operating in. Positive GEX = dampening, range-bound, mean-reverting. Negative GEX = amplifying, trending, volatile. Understanding where you are in this cycle is one of the most useful edges available to any serious options trader.

Dealer Positioning Decoded: How Probable GEX Is Calculated

OptionsFlow Dealer Flow 7 min read

The four GEX scenario charts give a range of possibilities for dealer positioning. But they all assume the same posture applies uniformly to every single strike on the chain. That assumption is too crude. In practice, a market maker might be long gamma at the 24,000 strike (because large institutional writers sold calls there) but short gamma at the 23,500 strike (because retail traders bought protective puts aggressively). The Probable GEX chart attempts to resolve this strike-by-strike.

The Core Question

When OI increases at a particular strike, the critical question is: Who opened that position? A writer selling calls to collect premium has the opposite P&L profile from a buyer purchasing calls for protection or direction. Crucially, from the market-maker perspective, these two transactions result in opposite gamma positions — one makes the dealer long gamma at that strike, the other makes them short.

The challenge is that the public OI data only tells you the quantity of contracts outstanding. It does not directly label each contract as "opened by a writer" or "opened by a buyer." Figuring that out requires reading multiple signals simultaneously.

The Five Signals We Use

For every OI change at every strike across every snapshot in the session, the analysis evaluates five pieces of evidence:

1. IV Direction (Primary Signal)

When a large institutional writer sells options, they are adding supply to the options market. Supply pushes prices down. Because options are priced in terms of implied volatility, more selling means IV falls or stays flat. When buyers are dominant, demand pushes IV up. So: OI rising + IV flat or falling = writers. OI rising + IV rising = buyers. This is the single strongest signal available.

2. GEX/OI Proportionality

Writers tend to sell near-the-money options where gamma is highest. Buyers tend to buy cheap OTM options where gamma per rupee of premium is lower. By comparing how much gamma changed relative to how much OI changed, we can distinguish high-gamma writer activity from low-gamma buyer activity.

3. Strike Moneyness × Market Direction

Context matters. OTM call OI rising while the market is also rising is consistent with call writers following the market up — a classic wall-building pattern. The same OTM call OI rising while the market is falling is more unusual and more likely to be buyers hedging a gap-up risk. The analysis uses the synthetic futures price as the best available estimate of market direction between snapshots.

4. Proximity to ATM

ATM strikes carry more informational weight. A large OI change at the ATM strike has a bigger impact on dealer gamma than the same OI change deep OTM. The analysis applies an ATM proximity multiplier so that near-money evidence counts for more in the final signal.

5. Recency Weighting

Not all historical snapshots are equally relevant. The most recent OI changes reflect the freshest positioning, while older snapshots reflect positions that may have been partially unwound or rolled. Each OI snapshot is weighted exponentially — the most recent gets full weight, and each prior snapshot receives 85% of the previous one's weight.

Signed Delta Accumulation: The Key Idea

This is where the method departs fundamentally from simpler approaches. Rather than labelling an entire strike as "dealer long" or "dealer short," the analysis tracks each OI increment individually and signs it based on the evidence at the time it was added.

Example: Over the course of a session, 2,000 lots of writer activity accumulate at the 24,000 call (dealer long gamma). Then in the last 30 minutes, 500 lots of buyer activity appear. The final result: the 2,000 writer lots retain their positive sign; only the 500 buyer lots are signed negative. Net: 1,500 lots dealer-long equivalent. The whole strike does not get relabelled just because some buyers arrived late.

This produces a per-strike signed GEX balance that respects the history of position-building rather than overwriting it with the most recent signal. The final Probable GEX bar for each strike is the current total GEX at that strike multiplied by this signed fraction.

Fallback for Low-History Strikes

For strikes with little or no OI change history — typically very far OTM or newly listed — the analysis falls back to a structural model that uses moneyness, strike-level PCR (put-call ratio), OI concentration, and market direction to estimate dealer positioning. This fallback is explicitly marked as lower-confidence and scaled down accordingly.

What the Chart Shows You

Positive (blue) bars: Dealer is estimated to be long gamma at this strike. Market-maker hedging will dampen moves in either direction away from this level.

Negative (orange) bars: Dealer is estimated to be short gamma at this strike. Moves through this level may be amplified.

The net total across all strikes tells you whether the aggregate dealer book is in a dampening or amplifying posture. Watch the peak absolute bar — that is where pin gravity is strongest. Watch for negative bars near the current spot price — those are structural vulnerabilities.

Vanna and Charm: The Greeks That Move Markets at Expiry

OptionsFlow Second-Order Greeks 6 min read

Gamma gets most of the attention in options education. But two second-order Greeks — Vanna and Charm — are responsible for some of the most reliable and systematic market-moving flows in the entire F&O calendar, particularly in the final hours of a weekly expiry cycle. Understanding them helps explain behaviour that otherwise looks completely unpredictable.

Vanna: Delta's Sensitivity to Volatility

Vanna measures how much an option's delta changes when implied volatility moves. If you hold a long call and IV rises, your delta increases (the call becomes more likely to expire in the money). If IV falls, your delta decreases.

For a market maker who is delta-neutral, this creates a problem: every time IV changes, their delta hedge ratio becomes stale. They must rebalance. This rebalancing is vanna-driven delta hedging — and because it is triggered by IV moves rather than spot moves, it often runs in a direction that surprises traders watching only price action.

The practical pattern looks like this: If IV drops sharply — say, after a Fed announcement resolves uncertainty — market makers who are long calls see their deltas shrink. They must sell futures or spot to rebalance. This selling happens even though the market itself may not have moved materially on price. To a pure price-chart watcher, the selling looks random. To someone tracking vanna exposure (VEX), it is completely logical.

Vanna Exposure (VEX) — What the Chart Shows

The VEX chart on this dashboard shows the total vanna exposure of the estimated dealer book, strike by strike. A large positive VEX number means: if IV falls, market makers will need to sell the underlying. A large negative VEX means: if IV falls, they will need to buy.

Rule of thumb: If VEX is large and positive, watch for selling pressure on any IV spike resolution (expiry, event clearance). If VEX is large and negative, watch for buying flows on the same occasions.

The most important time to monitor VEX is the final week before a major expiry. As options move from being OTM into ATM territory (or vice versa), their vanna values can change dramatically, forcing large hedge adjustments in a compressed time window.

Charm: Delta's Sensitivity to Time

Charm (sometimes called delta decay or "DdeltaDtime") measures how much an option's delta changes purely due to the passage of time, holding everything else constant. As an OTM option approaches expiry with the underlying price unchanged, its probability of expiring in the money decreases. Its delta therefore also decreases — toward zero. The market maker who sold this OTM call was buying spot to hedge the positive delta. As charm erodes that delta, they must now sell that spot back.

This is why you frequently see persistent selling pressure in OTM call strikes through Thursday and Friday of expiry week, even when Nifty isn't moving dramatically. It is charm unwind — market makers mechanically reducing their delta hedges as time value bleeds out of the options they've written.

Charm Exposure (CEX) — What the Chart Shows

The CEX chart aggregates charm across all positions in the estimated dealer book. A net positive charm number means that the aggregate dealer book will need to reduce long delta hedges as time passes — effectively selling pressure from charm. A net negative number means market makers will be adding to their hedges — effectively buying pressure from charm.

CEX tends to be most meaningful in the 2-5 days before expiry. On expiry day itself, charm flows can be dramatic and rapid, contributing to the "pin action" seen at major strikes.

The ITM VEX Chart

The ITM Vanna Exposure chart tracks something more specific: the net vanna from options that are already in the money. ITM options have higher deltas than OTM options, which means their vanna profiles are structurally different. This chart is particularly useful for understanding what happens to dealer hedges if the market makes a large, sustained directional move that brings a batch of OTM strikes into the money.

Think of it this way: if Nifty is at 24,000 and there is a large concentration of 23,500 calls (currently OTM) with high positive vanna, any rally that brings those calls ATM will trigger a wave of delta-hedge buying from market makers. The ITM VEX chart helps anticipate where those threshold effects are concentrated.

Why This Matters for Your Trading

Vanna and charm flows do not respond to news or sentiment. They are purely mechanical, time-and-vol-driven. They tend to be front-run by sophisticated participants who model them in advance. Understanding the direction and magnitude of these flows gives you a structural edge: you know roughly where the systematic selling or buying will come from before it happens, even if you don't know exactly when the trigger will fire.

Reading the Volatility Skew: What OTM Puts and Calls Are Really Saying

OptionsFlow Volatility 7 min read

In a textbook Black-Scholes world, options at different strikes but the same expiry would all trade at the same implied volatility. In the real world, they never do. The pattern of how IV varies across strikes — the volatility skew — is one of the richest sources of market intelligence available. It reflects everything from structural hedging demand to market fear to positioning crowding.

The Skew Table: What You're Looking At

The skew table on this dashboard is organised symmetrically around ATM. Each row corresponds to one OTM distance level — 1% through 5% away from current spot. The columns are: the put strike and its IV, the option delta at that strike, the Risk Reversal (RR) for that level, then the call delta, call IV, and call strike. The RR sits in the centre of each row so you can read the put/call asymmetry instantly.

This is what a normal, healthy equity skew looks like. OTM puts carry higher IV than equidistant OTM calls at every level. The RR column confirms this: it is negative throughout, widening as you go further OTM — the market pays more for downside protection the further away the strike is.

Why Delta Matters Here

Delta is the option market's own probability estimate: a Δ28 put has roughly a 28% chance of expiring in the money. It also tells you how aggressively the market maker must hedge. A Δ28 put requires the market maker to short 28 shares of underlying per 100 options to stay neutral. When you see the put delta significantly higher than the call delta at the same OTM distance, it means put IV is elevated — the market is pricing more probability into the downside strike than the symmetric upside equivalent. This asymmetry is the skew made visible in probability terms, not just in IV percentage points.

Key read: If Put Δ and Call Δ are close at 3% OTM (say Δ28 vs Δ26), skew is modest. If Put Δ is Δ35 while Call Δ is Δ22 at the same distance, the market is massively mispricing downside probability versus upside — fear is elevated, and put buyers are paying a significant premium.

The Risk Reversal (RR) Column

The Risk Reversal at each level is simply Put IV − Call IV at that OTM distance. In most equity index markets it is negative — puts are more expensive. A more negative RR means more fear of downside. A less negative or positive RR means the market is pricing upside risk — unusual, but it happens in strong bull phases or into positive catalysts.

Reading the RR across levels is as important as any single number. A RR that widens sharply from 1% to 3% (say −0.5% at 1% OTM to −4.5% at 3% OTM) means the smile is steep — the market is aggressively buying tail protection. A flat RR across all five levels means the smile is compressed — there is no particular fear of larger moves, and premium sellers may have a structural edge across all strikes.

Interpretation: RR of −3% to −5% at the 3–5% OTM level is normal for Indian indices. Tighter than −2% suggests complacency. Wider than −7% suggests institutional hedging surge or genuine fear. A positive RR (calls more expensive than puts at any level) is a structural signal of upside squeeze demand — rare, but significant.

What the OTM Curve Shape Tells You

Beyond individual RR numbers, the shape of the skew across the five levels contains structural information. A steep put wing — RR widening sharply row by row — suggests panic hedging, someone buying protection aggressively and pushing up wing prices. A flat put wing — RR barely changing as you go further OTM — suggests heavy writing at those strikes dampening the bid, often a sign of a range-bound, premium-selling regime.

On the call side, a flat or gently falling call wing is normal. If call IVs rise with strike (rare in Indian equities), the call delta at far strikes will be higher than expected — a squeeze-demand signal, potential early indicator of a directional breakout attempt with options buyers aggressively accumulating upside.

Why Puts Are Almost Always More Expensive Than Calls

There are two structural reasons for this. First, institutional investors — mutual funds, insurers, large proprietary desks — hold large long equity positions that they persistently hedge with downside puts. This structural buying keeps put IV elevated above what pure probability would justify. Second, the actual statistical evidence of fat-tailed downside returns in equities means the market assigns a higher probability to large down moves than a normal distribution suggests. This crash risk premium is priced into OTM puts directly, and it shows in both the IV column and the delta asymmetry.

Volatility Risk Premium (VRP)

The VRP measures the difference between implied volatility and realised volatility over a comparable period. When implied vol is higher than what the market actually delivered, the VRP is positive — option sellers earned a premium for risk that didn't fully materialise. When realised vol exceeds implied vol, the VRP is negative — option buyers received value.

Premium (positive VRP): IV is elevated relative to recent realised vol. The normal state — option sellers consistently collect a risk premium. A very high premium can indicate the market is overpaying for protection, a mean-reversion signal for IV sellers.

Discount (negative VRP): Realised vol is running above what was implied. The market under-priced the risk that occurred. Negative VRP periods are often associated with trending, volatile markets where directional option buyers fare well.

The Synthetic Futures Premium

The synthetic futures price is derived from put-call parity: it equals spot plus (call price − put price) for the same strike. In a fairly priced market it should be close to the futures price. Large divergences between synthetic futures and actual spot can indicate funding stress, dividend mispricing, or significant gamma imbalances. We use synthetic futures throughout the analysis as a more reliable reference point for ATM than spot price alone, because it reflects the actual forward price implied by the options market.

How to Use This Dashboard: A Walkthrough of Every Chart and Number

OptionsFlow User Guide 9 min read

This dashboard produces a lot of information across a lot of charts. The goal of this guide is to explain exactly what each piece is, why it's there, and how to read it in a logical sequence — moving from the broadest structural view down to specific actionable detail.

Step 1: Start With the Synopsis Card

The Synopsis is the first thing to read. It compresses the entire analysis into one screen. The key numbers to check first are:

  • GEX Regime: LONG γ means dampening, range-bound environment. SHORT γ means amplifying, trending conditions.
  • GEX Pin: The strike with maximum absolute gamma concentration. This is your magnetic level for the expiry cycle. Price tends to gravitate here, especially as expiry approaches.
  • Zero Gamma Line (ZGL): The price level where total dealer gamma flips from positive to negative (or vice versa). Moving above the ZGL in a long-gamma regime often accelerates upside. Breaking below in a short-gamma regime can trigger cascading selling.
  • Put Wall & Call Wall: These are the dominant structural support and resistance levels based on absolute OI. The Call Wall is the ceiling where call writers have concentrated their positions. The Put Wall is the floor where put writers are defending.
  • ATM IV & VRP: High IV + positive VRP (premium) = option sellers have an edge. Low IV + negative VRP (discount) = option buyers may have an edge.

Step 2: Read the OI & GEX Monitor

The OI Monitor chart shows the current option chain — call OI and put OI as stacked bars per strike, with GEX overlaid. This is your snapshot of current positioning. Look for:

  • Strike concentration: Where are the largest OI bars? These are structural levels the market is building around.
  • Asymmetry: Are calls or puts dominant in total OI? A heavy put side usually reflects institutional hedging. A heavy call side is less common and often indicates retail momentum or professional call writing.
  • GEX direction per strike: The GEX overlay shows whether gamma at each strike is positive (dealer long, dampening) or negative (dealer short, amplifying). Negative GEX near spot is a structural warning.

Step 3: Understand Dealer Positioning via Probable GEX

The Probable GEX chart is the most sophisticated output on the dashboard. Instead of assuming all market makers are in the same posture, it estimates the signed dealer position strike-by-strike based on how OI has been building during the session.

Blue bars (positive) = dealer estimated long gamma at that strike. Orange bars (negative) = dealer estimated short gamma. The taller the bar, the more conviction behind the estimate.

The net total at the top tells you the aggregate regime. Pay attention to the pattern around the current spot price: if the nearest strikes all show positive (long gamma), you are in a structurally dampened range. If they show negative (short gamma), nearby moves may be self-reinforcing.

Step 4: Check GEX Exposure Scenarios

The 4-scenario GEX chart presents the full option chain gamma under each of the four dealer positioning assumptions. Reading this alongside Probable GEX helps you understand the range of outcomes: the probable chart tells you what is most likely, the scenario charts tell you what the extremes look like.

Step 5: Evaluate Vanna and Charm for Flow Timing

The VEX chart tells you which way IV-driven flows will push the market. If the session sees an IV crush (common after a resolution of uncertainty), will dealers be forced to sell or buy? VEX tells you.

The CEX chart tells you which way time-driven flows will push. As time decays out of the options, will dealers reduce long hedges (sell pressure) or reduce short hedges (buy pressure)? CEX tells you.

Both are most relevant in the 2-5 days before expiry. Outside of that window, their flows are smaller and less likely to dominate price action.

Step 6: Read the Volatility Skew Table

The OTM skew table in the Synopsis shows, for each 1–5% OTM level: the put strike and its IV, the option delta at that strike, the Risk Reversal (RR) — put IV minus call IV — and the mirror call data. The RR column sits in the centre so you can read put/call asymmetry at a glance. A widening negative RR as you move further OTM means the market is pricing tail fear progressively — steep downside hedging demand. A flat or narrowing RR means the smile is compressed and premium sellers may have a structural edge across strikes. Delta at each level tells you the market's implied probability of reaching that strike — a Δ28 put at 3% OTM is pricing roughly a 28% chance of a 3% down move by expiry, which you can compare directly against your own expectation.

Step 7: Use the PCR and Top GEX Tables as Confirmation

The Put-Call Ratio (PCR) table shows the ratio of put OI to call OI across different strike ranges. PCR above 1.0 means more puts outstanding than calls — typically a bullish signal (more put writing/hedging, which implies structural support below). PCR below 1.0 means more calls — can be neutral or slightly bearish depending on context. The near-ATM PCR is most meaningful for short-term direction.

The Top 6 GEX Strikes table shows the six strikes with the largest absolute gamma concentration. These are your key structural levels for the expiry cycle — important for setting up condors, straddles, or defining risk in directional trades.

Putting the Signals Together: A Practical Example

Imagine you see: GEX Regime = LONG γ, GEX Pin = 24,000, current Nifty at 23,950. Probable GEX shows strong positive bars at 23,800, 24,000 and 24,200. VRP is positive (option sellers earning a premium). PCR is 1.2 (moderately bullish structure). The skew table shows normal put wing (Δ ATM around +3% at 5% OTM) and slightly falling call wing.

This composite picture says: the market is in a structurally dampened state with a clear gravitational centre at 24,000. Short-gamma amplification is not the current risk. The option seller has a structural edge. The instrument is not pricing an unusual amount of tail fear in either direction. A range-bound, premium-selling approach (iron condor, short strangle around the pin) fits the structural environment well.

Now imagine VEX is large and positive, and you're three days before expiry. You know that any IV compression event will trigger dealer selling. You might use this to anticipate that a sharp rally (which often compresses IV) could quickly reverse — not because of fundamentals, but because of mechanical vanna-driven selling pressure that kicks in as soon as the fear premium comes out of the options.

Final Notes

All outputs on this dashboard are derived from publicly available NSE option chain data and the standard Black-Scholes model. They are descriptive of structural forces, not directional predictions. The market can and does move against structural forces — especially when a macro catalyst overwhelms local dealer flows. Think of this data as describing the terrain the market is travelling through, not a map that tells you which direction it will go.
